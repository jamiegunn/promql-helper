Investigations

What each question checks

Every panel carries a rule that turns its numbers into a sentence. Those rules are listed here in full, because a threshold you cannot see is a threshold you cannot argue with.

How a finding is made

Each panel runs its queries, then evaluates the result against a threshold and produces a severity plus one sentence. The report's verdict is the worst finding across all panels, not a summary or an average — averaging would hide the one panel you need to see.

A panel is skipped entirely if the metrics it needs are absent, and appears in the gap list at the bottom of the report instead. That is why two services can produce reports of very different lengths.

These are defaults, not your SLOs

The thresholds below are reasonable for a user-facing web service. They are wrong for a batch job, a video encoder, or a report generator where a 30-second p99 is entirely normal. Read a finding as "here is something unusual, by general standards" rather than as a judgement about your system. They are not yet configurable.

Is this service serving happy users?

The RED method — rate, errors, duration. Between them these describe the experience your users are actually having, rather than how the machine feels.

What it needs

An HTTP server request counter, and ideally a latency histogram. Recognised from Micrometer, OpenTelemetry (current and legacy conventions), and the Prometheus client libraries. Without histogram buckets you still get traffic and error rates, but no percentiles — that is the single most common gap.

What it checks

Check Finding at
Failure rate Acting up at ≥ 5% of requests returning 5xx · Worth a look at ≥ 1%
99th percentile latency Acting up at ≥ 3s · Worth a look at ≥ 1s
Tail spread Worth a look when p99 is ≥ 20× p50
Failures by endpoint Acting up when one endpoint exceeds 5% failures for more than a tenth of the window; Worth a look for a shorter spike
Slowest endpoint Needs attention at ≥ 3s p99 · Worth a look at ≥ 1s
Peak vs typical load Worth a look when peak traffic is ≥ 5× the average

Reading the tail spread. A p99 twenty times the median means most requests are fine and a specific subset is not. Look for one slow endpoint, one slow replica, or lock contention — not for a general slowdown.

How much CPU and memory does it use?

Sizing and saturation. A service that averages half a core but peaks at three is a very different capacity problem from one sitting flat at one and a half, and an average cannot tell you which you have.

What it needs

cAdvisor for container CPU and memory, and kube-state-metrics for the configured limits. These scrape under their own jobs, so the app identifies your pods from the namespace and pod labels on the application's own series. If your metrics carry no such labels, these checks are reported as gaps rather than guessed at. Where cAdvisor is unavailable it falls back to process_cpu_seconds_total and process_resident_memory_bytes, which show usage but not limits.

What it checks

Check Finding at
CPU percentiles Worth a look when peak CPU is ≥ 4× the median
CPU against limit Acting up at ≥ 95% · Worth a look at ≥ 80% · flagged as oversized below 20%
CPU throttling Acting up at ≥ 25% of scheduling periods · Worth a look at ≥ 5%
Memory against limit Acting up at ≥ 90% · Worth a look at ≥ 75%
Memory trend Worth a look when every replica grew ≥ 25% from the start of the window to the end
Uneven replicas Worth a look when the busiest is ≥ 3× the average
Restarts Acting up at ≥ 3 in the window · Worth a look at ≥ 1

Throttling is the one to watch. It explains latency that the CPU graph alone cannot: a container can sit at 40% average CPU and still be parked by the kernel during every burst. Unlike memory, exceeding a CPU limit is not fatal — it is just slow, silently.

Is the Java runtime itself healthy?

When a Java service is slow the cause is usually that it is collecting garbage, out of heap, or blocked on locks. All three look identical from outside — the requests are just slow — and completely different from inside the runtime.

What it needs

JVM metrics from Micrometer, the Prometheus Java client, or the OpenTelemetry JVM instrumentation. In Spring Boot these come free with spring-boot-starter-actuator.

What it checks

Check Finding at
Heap in use Acting up at ≥ 90% of max heap · Worth a look at ≥ 75%
GC overhead Acting up at ≥ 10% of wall-clock time · Worth a look at ≥ 5%
Heap trend Worth a look when the post-collection low point rises by ≥ 15 percentage points across the window
Average GC pause Acting up at ≥ 1s · Worth a look at ≥ 200ms
Collection frequency Worth a look at ≥ 2 collections per second
Blocked threads Needs attention at ≥ 10 threads BLOCKED · Worth a look at ≥ 1
Thread count Worth a look when the peak is ≥ 2× the average
Metaspace Worth a look above 512 MB

A healthy heap is a sawtooth. It fills, a collection frees it, it fills again. A leak shows up in the troughs rising, not the peaks — which is why the heap trend check looks at the low points rather than the maximum.

Is this app pummelling the database?

Deliberately looks from both ends. The connection pool tells you whether your threads are waiting; the database server tells you whether it is struggling. They disagree more often than people expect.

What it needs

Connection-pool metrics from HikariCP, Spring JDBC or Tomcat JDBC on the application side. Optionally a postgres_exporter or mysqld_exporter job for the server side, chosen from the dropdown on the card.

What it checks

Check Finding at
Pool utilisation Acting up at ≥ 90% · Worth a look at ≥ 70% · Needs attention if it ever hits 99%
Threads waiting Acting up at ≥ 5 queued · Worth a look at ≥ 1
Connection timeouts Acting up at ≥ 1 in the window
Time acquiring a connection Needs attention at ≥ 100ms · Worth a look at ≥ 10ms
Query time Needs attention at ≥ 1s average · Worth a look at ≥ 100ms
Server connections Acting up at ≥ 90% of max_connections · Worth a look at ≥ 70%
Buffer cache hit ratio Worth a look below 90%
Deadlocks Needs attention at ≥ 1 in the window

Threads waiting is the clearest single signal. A pool at 100% with nothing queued is fine — it is fully utilised. A pool with threads queued means requests are doing nothing but waiting, and raising the pool size usually fixes it outright. Check the server-side panels before you do: if the database is also near its connection limit, a bigger pool makes things worse.

Is this app hammering Redis?

Redis is almost never slow on its own. When it looks slow it is usually the application calling it far more than it needs to, one expensive command blocking the single thread, or memory pressure quietly evicting the keys the cache exists to hold.

What it needs

A redis_exporter job for the server side. Application-side latency comes from Lettuce or the OpenTelemetry client instrumentation, and is worth having — comparing the two tells you whether time is going into Redis or into the network and client queue.

What it checks

Check Finding at
Cache hit ratio Needs attention below 50% · Worth a look below 80%
Memory against maxmemory Acting up at ≥ 90% · Worth a look at ≥ 75% · flagged if no limit is set at all
Evictions Acting up at ≥ 100 keys/sec · Worth a look at any eviction
Blocking commands Needs attention when KEYS, FLUSHALL, SMEMBERS, HGETALL or SORT exceed 0.1 calls/sec
Time per command Needs attention at ≥ 10ms inside Redis
Client-side latency Needs attention at ≥ 50ms as measured by the app
Rejected connections Acting up at ≥ 1 in the window
Connected clients Worth a look above 5,000
Command throughput Worth a look above 50,000 commands/sec

Redis commands are measured in microseconds. Milliseconds means the command is scanning a large structure, and while it does, every other client waits behind it. That is why the blocking-command check fires at a rate as low as one call every ten seconds.
